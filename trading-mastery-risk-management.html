Most trading guides start with charts. This one starts with your paycheck. The traders who survive long enough to get good are usually the ones who never put essential money at risk and who decided how much they could lose before they placed a trade.

Shader7 does not offer a trading platform or trading signals. What it does offer are free tools that answer the questions that come first: how much do I really earn, how much can I spare, and what is that worth in the currency my broker uses? This guide connects those answers to the core rules of position sizing.

This article is for education only and is not financial advice. Trading involves a real risk of losing money. Check the rules for your country and consider talking to a licensed adviser.

Step 1: Decide your risk capital from real income

Risk capital is money you could lose completely without affecting rent, bills, debt payments or your emergency fund.

  1. Find your real monthly income. If you work shifts, log the month in the Hourly Salary Calculator: base salary, base hours, weekend, night and C-shift multipliers, and your daily hours. It shows your gross monthly salary; subtract tax and deductions to get take-home pay.
  2. Budget the essentials first. Use the steps in the monthly budgeting guide, and use FairShare to know your exact share of household costs.
  3. Build an emergency fund of at least three months of essential costs before trading.
  4. Only what is left over can become risk capital, and only part of it.

A useful rule of thumb: if losing the whole trading account would change how you live next month, the account is too big.

Step 2: Work in your broker's currency

If your income is in RON or INR but your broker account or instrument is in USD or EUR, convert before you size a trade. The Hourly Salary Calculator includes a Currency Converter with daily reference rates from the Frankfurter API. Your broker's actual rate and fees will differ slightly, so round your risk down after converting.

Step 3: The 1–2% rule

Risk no more than 1–2% of your trading account on any single trade. "Risk" means the amount you lose if your stop-loss is hit, not the size of the position.

Why it matters: with 1% risk, ten losses in a row (which happens to every trader) cost about 10% of the account. With 10% risk, the same streak leaves you with about a third of your account and needs a gain of nearly 190% to recover.

Loss in a row Account left at 1% risk At 2% risk At 10% risk
5 95.1% 90.4% 59.0%
10 90.4% 81.7% 34.9%
20 81.8% 66.8% 12.2%

Step 4: Set the stop-loss where the idea is wrong

Place your stop-loss at the price that proves your trade idea wrong: for example below a recent swing low for a long trade, or above a swing high for a short. Do not set it at an arbitrary percentage just to make the position bigger.

Step 5: Size the position

Amount at risk  = Account size × Risk %
Risk per unit   = |Entry price − Stop-loss price|
Position size   = Amount at risk ÷ Risk per unit

Example: account ₹1,00,000, risk 1% (₹1,000). You buy at ₹500 with a stop at ₹480, so risk per share is ₹20.

Position size = ₹1,000 ÷ ₹20 = 50 shares (₹25,000 position). If the stop is hit, you lose about ₹1,000, which is 1% of the account, plus fees and any slippage.

If the stop were at ₹490 (₹10 risk), the position would be 100 shares. A tighter stop gives a bigger position for the same risk, which is why stop placement must come from the chart, not from wanting a bigger trade.

Position sizing matrix (₹1,00,000 account, ₹1,000 risk)

Stop distance per share Shares Position value at ₹500
₹5 200 ₹1,00,000
₹10 100 ₹50,000
₹20 50 ₹25,000
₹50 20 ₹10,000

Watch leverage: if a position is close to your whole account, a gap past your stop can cost far more than planned.

Step 6: Risk-to-reward and win rate

Risk-to-reward (R:R) compares the planned loss with the planned gain. A 1:2 trade risks ₹1,000 to make ₹2,000.

The win rate you need just to break even (before costs) is:

Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R)
R:R Break-even win rate
1:1 50%
1:2 33%
1:3 25%

Higher R:R lowers the win rate you need, but targets further away are hit less often. Track both honestly.

Step 7: Write a trading plan

Before your first trade, write down:

  • Your risk capital and the risk per trade (for example 1%).
  • Which markets and timeframes you trade.
  • Your entry rules and where stops go.
  • Your minimum R:R.
  • A daily loss limit (for example 3%) and a monthly loss limit (for example 6%) after which you stop and review.
  • How you record each trade: date, instrument, entry, stop, target, size, result and a note on why.

Psychological traps

  • Moving the stop further away to avoid a loss.
  • Revenge trading to win back a loss immediately.
  • Increasing size after a winning streak.
  • Trading money meant for bills. Step 1 exists to prevent this.

Pre-trade checklist

  1. Is this money risk capital, separate from essentials and emergency savings?
  2. Is the stop-loss at a logical invalidation point?
  3. Is the risk 1–2% of the account or less, in the account's currency?
  4. Is the R:R at or above your minimum?
  5. Am I within my daily and monthly loss limits?
  6. Have I recorded the plan before entering?

Keeping a trading journal

A journal turns losses into lessons. For every trade, record:

Field Example
Date and time 2026-11-19 10:15
Instrument ABC Ltd
Direction Long
Entry, stop, target 500 / 480 / 540
Position size 50 shares
Planned risk ₹1,000 (1%)
Planned R:R 1:2
Result +₹2,000 (+2R)
Followed the plan? Yes
Notes Entered on breakout retest

Review the journal monthly. Measure results in R (multiples of your planned risk) as well as money; it shows whether your method works regardless of account size.

When to stop trading

  • You hit your daily or monthly loss limit.
  • You are tempted to use money from your essentials or emergency fund.
  • You notice you are moving stops, skipping the journal or trading out of boredom or revenge.
  • Your income changes. Re-run Step 1 with your new monthly figures before you trade again.

Frequently asked questions

Does Shader7 have a trading or position-size calculator?

No. Shader7 offers money tools such as the Hourly Salary Calculator, its currency converter and FairShare, which help you set risk capital from your real income. Position sizing uses the simple formula in this guide.

How much of my salary should I trade with?

Only money left after essential costs, debt payments and an emergency fund, and only an amount you could lose completely without changing how you live.

Why risk only 1–2% per trade?

Losing streaks are normal. Small risk per trade keeps a streak survivable and leaves you able to keep trading your plan.

Is the currency converter accurate enough for trading?

It uses daily reference rates, which are fine for planning. Your broker's live rate and fees will differ, so round your risk down after converting.

Is this financial advice?

No. This guide is educational. Trading carries a real risk of loss; consider professional advice for your situation.
